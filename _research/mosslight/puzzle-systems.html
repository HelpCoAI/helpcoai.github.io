Mosslight · puzzle systems research · August 2026

The Wants Board

You asked for addictive puzzles to stretch the game across five islands. The research says the puzzle you want is already sitting in your game — it's the decorating — but that turning it into a puzzle takes a different mechanism than the obvious one, and the obvious one quietly fails in a game you can undo.

16 research + design agents7 topic sweeps2 competing designs

The one-paragraph version

Make every creature's residency a printed checklist of spatial requirements evaluated against the decorations you place — Viva Piñata's model. The puzzle is never what is required; it's how to arrange one island that satisfies a dozen creatures whose demands contradict each other on finite land. That is unfailable, it generates its own difficulty from geometry, it makes five islands mechanically necessary rather than just bigger, and it is roughly sixty spreadsheet rows rather than sixty authored levels.

01

The trap in the obvious answer

Five of my seven research agents independently recommended Dorfromantik-style placement scoring: give every decoration an adjacency score, show the number, let optimising it be the puzzle. It's the consensus answer and it is almost right.

The design agent that disagreed found the flaw, and I think it's correct.

Dorfromantik's placement is a genuine puzzle because of two rules it enforces: a finite tile stack that acts as a clock, and irreversibility — once placed, a tile stays. Take away either one and the puzzle collapses.

Mosslight is a persistent island you live on. Players will expect free relocation and unlimited undo, and they should have both — Islanders' no-demolish rule is a roguelike device that reads as punishment in a home. But a global additive placement score with free undo degenerates into something that isn't a puzzle at all: you nudge the bench two tiles left, watch the number, nudge it back. That's hill-climbing on a gradient. It's mildly pleasant, it's genuinely good feedback, and it will not occupy anyone for forty hours.

The distinction that resolves it

Keep placement scoring — as feedback, not as the puzzle. A ghosted preview showing the delta before you commit is one of the best feel mechanics available and it costs a scoring function. Just don't mistake the number going up for a challenge.

02

Failable is the real enemy, not gated

The gating research came back with a firm rule: never put a puzzle on the critical path, put them in optional off-path chambers like Breath of the Wild's shrines. Churn concentrates exactly at gates, and your next island is your most valuable asset — don't hold it hostage.

Half right. The distinction being elided isn't gated versus ungated. It's failable versus unfailable.

Viva Piñata gates every single species behind a requirement checklist, and it's the beloved ancestor of this whole genre. You cannot fail a checklist. You can only be not-done-yet. The requirements are printed openly and there are thousands of valid arrangements. Meanwhile an off-path shrine is a separate verb in a separate room — precisely the tone break and context switch that the same body of research condemns everywhere else.

So: puzzles go on the critical path, and nothing on the critical path can be failed, timed, or single-solution.

03

The system

S1The spine — build first

The Wants Board

A creature arrives at the island edge, points at a patch of ground, and tells you in plain printed language exactly what it needs to see around that spot before it moves in. You satisfy it by decorating. No second verb, no second screen, no way to fail.

Home site
Every creature claims one point on the ground, and every requirement is evaluated within a stated radius of it. This is the most important decision in the design: it bounds the puzzle to an 8–16m circle rather than the whole island, so it's legible, solvable in one sitting, and drawable as a single ring. A cheap craftable Den Marker lets the player offer an alternative site — that's the agency valve against bad placement.
Predicates
About 14 templates, each a cheap spatial query over placed objects and terrain: COUNT(tag, R) ≥ N · COUNT(tag, R) ≤ N · DISTINCT(family, R) ≥ K · NEAR(terrain, D) · FAR(tag, D) · OPEN_GROUND(≥ area) · CANOPY · ELEVATION · SIGHTLINE · LIGHT(hour) · RESIDENT(species) · ABSENT(species) · CO_RESIDENT · CORRIDOR
Progression
Four states per species — Sighted → Visiting → Resident → Bonded. Each transition is a checklist of 3–5 predicates. The moment the last box goes green, the transition fires.
Why it fits
It consumes your existing decoration catalogue as its entire puzzle vocabulary. Every decorative asset you've already made — and every one you add later — becomes new puzzle vocabulary for every species simultaneously. Content compounds instead of accumulating.
Cost
Predicate engine, spatial query cache, journal UI, backwards generator, state machine: roughly 120–180 hours. Then about 20 minutes of data entry per species. Sixty species is sixty puzzles from one build.
S2The difficulty engine

Crowding & Contested Ground

Land is finite and creatures' circles overlap. The lantern the ember-fox demands is the lantern the moth-hare refuses to live beside. No arrangement houses everyone — so the puzzle stops being "satisfy the checklist" and becomes decide who this island is for.

Territory
Every resident claims a disc of radius 4–14m by size tier. Discs may not overlap by more than 40%; the game refuses an illegal home site and offers the nearest legal alternative. That one rule creates a visible packing problem on finite land. Critically, it constrains home sites only — the player must never be told where they may not put a bench.
Won'ts
Wants are checked to reach residency. Won'ts are evaluated forever. Violating one raises Unrest 0→100 over three in-game days.
Telegraphing
No text needed. At Unrest 30 an icon appears. At 60 the creature walks to the offending object and stares at it. The player learns the cause by watching — free legibility, and pure charm. Your existing pet AI already does walk-to-node-and-do-a-thing.
The cozy-safe loss
At 100 the creature packs up and returns to the island edge with its wants preserved, ready to be re-satisfied. It never dies, is never lost, nothing is destroyed.
Cost
~30–40 hours. Conflict tables are generated, not authored. This is effectively free difficulty for every species already in the game.
S3Highest leverage per hour

Morph Expression

The arrangement doesn't just attract the creature — it authors what the creature looks like. Raise a fox in a shaded, water-heavy, lantern-free hollow and you get the Dusk morph. Raise one on sun-warmed high ground thick with flowers and you get Ember.

Rules
When a creature becomes Resident, snapshot an environment vector of its home site: dominant hue, canopy fraction, water fraction, elevation band, night light level, dominant material, object density. Seven dimensions, each in 3–4 buckets. Each species has a morph table of 4–8 target vectors, each with a visual delta — palette swap, pattern mask, maybe one small accessory mesh. Nearest vector wins.
The almanac
Shows every undiscovered morph as a silhouette plus one honest environmental hint: "Ember: sun-warmed, high ground, little water." That single line is simultaneously a collection goal, a knowledge puzzle, and a decoration brief.
Why it matters
Collection completionism is the actual endgame of this genre — around 30% of long-term retention. This makes it combinatorial rather than finite, and fills it by decorating differently instead of grinding. It is the cheapest content in the entire design.
Cost
~40–60 hours for the vector snapshot, morph tables, matching and almanac UI. Then 2–4 hours of palette work per morph.
S4Later — expensive but it's your marketing

Visitor Sightlines

Visitors don't score your island from orbit. They walk a route and score what they can actually see from where they stand.

Rules
A visitor spawns at the dock and walks a procedural route, stopping at 8–20 viewpoints. At each, cast a 60° fan of ~24 rays at two eye heights and collect the first object each ray hits, with distance. Score = appeal × distance falloff, with a novelty cap — each object type counts fully once per viewpoint and 30% for repeats, which kills prop-spam smoothly. Composition bonuses fall out of the same ray data: framing, depth across three distance bands, horizon, and a tidiness penalty for a visible workshop within 8m.
Why bother
It scores composition — the thing that actually makes an island beautiful, and the thing a global adjacency score cannot see. It also can't be gamed by nudging one object. And the postcard it produces is your store page.
Cost
~60–90 hours. Reuses your pathfinding and visitor NPCs. Adds no art.
S5The habit layer — build last

The Daily Plot

One 12×12m walled garden per day, identical for every player worldwide, seeded from the calendar date. Three creatures with generated wants, exactly 18 objects, full catalogue access inside the plot, no economy, no clock, no lives, no leaderboard.

The catch
All three creatures cannot be satisfied with 18 objects. Two of three is a good day. So the daily is a miniature crowding puzzle that teaches S2 painlessly.
Rewards
One decor token and a streak counter. Cosmetic only. Everything obtainable here is also obtainable through normal play, more slowly. Fully skippable, nothing gated behind it.
Cost
~30–50 hours once S1 exists — it's the Wants Board with the island removed. Infinite content thereafter: one integer per day.
04

Five islands, one vocabulary

Kishōtenketsu applied to one predicate vocabulary rather than five hand-built mechanics. Each island turns on two or three new predicate templates and one new scoring rule. Island five adds nothing new at all — it's a victory lap where everything combines.

IslandRoleNew predicatesThe lesson
1 · Home CoveIntroduceCOUNT≥, COUNT≤, NEAR(water), FAR(path)Placing a pretty thing makes a creature move in. 6 species, generous thresholds, no Won'ts, crowding disabled, land abundant.
2 · Terraced HillsDevelopDISTINCT, CANOPY, ELEVATIONVary your palette, and hide the workshop. First Won'ts. Terracing makes geometry matter before scarcity does. Morph hints appear.
3 · Lantern ReefThe turnSIGHTLINE, LIGHT(hour)You cannot have everyone. Land is genuinely scarce, crowding binds for the first time, species-vs-species exclusion appears. The signature conflict: a lantern one species requires and another forbids, with no room to separate them.
4 · The TangleCombineCO_RESIDENT, CORRIDORWants reference species living on other islands, so the archipelago becomes one board and the ferry becomes a puzzle move. Finished islands turn back into live puzzle space.
5 · —MasteryNoneNo new rules. Density, conflict and combination only.

Island 3 is where the game becomes a puzzle rather than a garden, and it's deliberately the same change of gear in every system at once, so it reads as one turn rather than three unrelated difficulty bumps.

How islands actually unlock

Two unfailable accumulating axes, both permanently visible, with an NPC naming the exact deficit in plain words — Animal Crossing's two-threshold model, but transparent. Residents (count of creatures housed) and Charm (the sightline score). Both must clear, so neither can be brute-forced.

Then ship key surplus: at every gate make meaningfully more species eligible than the threshold requires — target 1.5×. Slime Rancher shipped 12 keys for 11 gates specifically so that no unlock order could ever strand a player, and Disney Dreamlight Valley's players hit exactly that wall by unlocking biomes in the wrong order. No specific creature, morph, or daily result may ever be a prerequisite for anything.

05

The rules I'd hold hardest

  1. Nothing on the critical path can be failed. Unfailable checklists are fine and are exactly what Viva Piñata did. Anything with a lose condition concentrates churn precisely at the gate.
  2. No timers, move limits, energy meters, durability, or reflex checks. Anywhere, but least of all on gathering, which repeats thousands of times. Spiritfarer's timed mining is that game's most-complained-about verb; ACNH tool durability is the most-hated feature in the franchise's most successful entry, five years on.
  3. Hide nothing. Print every requirement, rubric and threshold in-game with live checkboxes. Viva Piñata printed everything and reads as beloved; Terraria hid its NPC happiness tables and the community plays it on spreadsheets; ACNH's opaque star rating is a documented five-year frustration.
  4. Never hand-author puzzles. Authored content runs roughly 50:1 to 300:1 build-hours to play-minutes for a solo dev and has near-zero replay. Every system here is a rules engine plus a config table for exactly this reason.
  5. No separate puzzle screen. No match-3, no merge board, no sliding tiles, no fishing minigame. If it isn't the placement and arrangement the player already does, it's a context switch out of the cozy state.
  6. Never emit a target the player's catalogue can't satisfy. Validate every generated want against unlocked items and regenerate. Terra Nil shipped mathematically impossible procedural coverage targets and players lost hours to them.
  7. Ship an unscored mode from the first hour. Toukana added exactly this to Dorfromantik after players said score pressure broke the coziness. Tiny Glade sold ~616k copies in a month at 97% positive with no goals at all — that's your null hypothesis, and every puzzle has to be something the player chooses to pull rather than something the game pushes.
  8. Nothing required behind daily login. Cozy Grove's soft wall is that game's most-cited complaint.
  9. Don't let Unrest accrue while the player is away from that island. Returning to a solved island that has unsolved itself is the fastest way to make this feel hostile.
  10. Redundant channels always. Never colour alone for predicate icons, satisfied states, or territory discs — shape plus icon plus position.
06

What this actually buys you

The honest number first: as a separate system, puzzles are worth about 10–15% of long-term retention in this genre. If you spend six months building puzzles instead of catalogue, decoration variety and creature variants, you will have optimised the smallest term in the equation.

Why the design above sidesteps that number

The 10–15% figure applies to puzzles as a separate system — and this design is built so no such system exists. The Wants Board is simultaneously the puzzle, the decoration goal, the creature-acquisition mechanic, and the reason to place any given object. It isn't competing for the 10% slot; it's the delivery mechanism for the 30% and the 25%. Morph Expression does the same trick to the catalogue.

If you cut this to one thing: ship the Wants Board plus Morphs and nothing else, and it would still be the most retentive version of your game.

Where I'd spend the marginal hour instead

Not on puzzle rules. On the feel of placing an object — the snap, the click, the ghost preview, the settle. Across Resident Evil 4's attaché case, Townscaper, Save Room and Tiny Glade, the reported satisfaction consistently lives in the placement feel rather than the rules. A great rule set with mushy placement feels like homework. A mediocre rule set with perfect placement feel sold 616,000 copies in a month with no goals at all.

07

Build order

  1. Placement feel — ghost preview, snap, settle. Before any rules.
  2. The predicate engine and journal UI (S1). Six species, four predicate types, one island. This is the whole bet; find out early whether reading a checklist and arranging a garden is fun for you.
  3. Crowding (S2) — the difficulty engine, and cheap once S1 exists.
  4. Morphs (S3) — the collection endgame, highest leverage per hour on the list.
  5. Sightlines (S4) and the Daily Plot (S5), in whichever order the game needs.

Step 2 is the real gate. Build one island with six species and no crowding, play it for a week, and ask whether you reach for it. If arranging a garden against a printed checklist isn't compelling at that scale, more predicates won't rescue it.
