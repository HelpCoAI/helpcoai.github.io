# 02 — The Value Equation

## The formula

Perceived value is not a feeling you create with adjectives. It is the output of four
variables, and you can move each one deliberately:

```
                Dream Outcome  ×  Perceived Likelihood of Achievement
   Value  =  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
                  Time Delay   ×   Effort & Sacrifice
```

Two variables above the line to maximize. Two below the line to minimize. That's the
whole thing.

The reason this is useful rather than merely tidy: when a prospect doesn't buy, the
failure is always traceable to one of these four. You don't need to guess. You diagnose
which term is weak and you fix that one.

## The four variables

### 1. Dream Outcome (maximize)

The state the prospect actually wants — not the deliverable you provide. Nobody wants a
CRM; they want to stop losing track of money owed to them. Nobody wants an AI
receptionist; they want to stop lying awake wondering how many jobs walked.

Two failure modes:

- **Selling the mechanism instead of the outcome.** Features are evidence for the
  outcome, not the outcome. Lead with the destination, use the mechanism as proof.
- **Aiming too low.** Founders systematically understate the dream outcome because
  they're afraid of over-promising. There is a real line at dishonesty, but most
  businesses are nowhere near it — they're hedging so hard the prospect can't tell what
  they'd actually get.

Dream outcome also has a status dimension that people under-use. What does having this
solved make them *to other people*? The operator who never misses a call is the one
whose reviews say "answered on the first ring at 9pm."

### 2. Perceived Likelihood of Achievement (maximize)

Will it work **for me specifically**? This is usually the real objection hiding behind
price objections, and it's the variable most businesses neglect entirely.

Levers that move it:

- **Proof, ranked by power:** third-party results > your own documented results >
  demonstration > testimonial > claim. A screenshot of a real outcome outperforms any
  sentence you can write about it.
- **Specificity.** Round numbers read as invented. "$43,700 in recovered jobs across
  nine months" is more believable than "over $40k."
- **Similarity.** The prospect must see *themselves* in the proof. One case study from
  their exact trade in their exact county beats fifty from elsewhere.
- **Guarantees.** These are pure likelihood-boosters — they transfer risk from the buyer
  to you. (See [03](03-grand-slam-offer.md).)
- **Naming the mechanism.** A named, explicable process ("the 60-Second Recovery
  Sequence") reads as more likely to work than an unnamed one, because it implies
  something repeatable rather than improvised.
- **Reducing the claim.** Counterintuitively, promising less but guaranteeing it harder
  often raises value, because the multiplication is against a bigger likelihood term.

### 3. Time Delay (minimize)

How long between paying and getting the outcome. This has two distinct components, and
conflating them is a common error:

- **Time to first perceptible result.** Almost always the more important one. The buyer
  needs a dopamine hit early — something that tells them they made a good decision. This
  is where churn is won or lost.
- **Time to full outcome.** Matters for the promise, but a distant full outcome is
  survivable if the first result comes fast.

So: engineer an early win into the delivery on purpose. Ask what you can make happen in
the first 48 hours, then build the onboarding around producing that thing. Long-cycle
outcomes should be broken into visible milestones so the perceived delay shrinks even
when the actual delay doesn't.

### 4. Effort & Sacrifice (minimize)

Everything the buyer must do, give up, learn, or risk. Includes the obvious (time,
labor, learning curve) and the non-obvious (changing how they already work, admitting to
staff they were wrong, abandoning a tool they championed, looking foolish if it fails).

This is where "done for you" earns its premium over "done with you" over "do it
yourself" — the identical outcome at three effort levels supports three prices, and the
lowest-effort version can often be priced at a large multiple of the highest-effort one.

The sharpest question to ask here: **what is the thing they have to do that they're
quietly dreading?** Remove that, and price rises without changing anything else.

## Using it as a diagnostic

When something isn't selling, walk the four terms in order:

| Symptom | Likely weak term | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "Sounds nice, but I'm fine" | Dream Outcome | You're selling the mechanism, or aiming too low. Raise the stakes; quantify the cost of the status quo. |
| "How do I know this works?" / stalls after a good call | Likelihood | Add proof from their exact segment. Add a guarantee. Name the mechanism. |
| "Let's revisit next quarter" | Time Delay | Manufacture and promise a fast first win. Show the week-one milestone. |
| "I don't have the bandwidth right now" | Effort & Sacrifice | Move further toward done-for-you. Remove the setup burden explicitly. |
| "It's too expensive" | Any of the four | Price objections are usually value objections wearing a disguise. Find which term is actually weak before you discount. |

That last row is the most valuable line in this file. Discounting in response to a price
objection treats the symptom and destroys margin. In most cases the prospect is telling
you the *likelihood* term is low — they'd pay the price if they believed it would work.

## Applied to HelpCo AI

Scoring the current positioning against the four terms:

**Dream Outcome — currently underplayed.** The site talks about missed calls, which is
the *mechanism of loss*, not the dream. The dream is: *every job that tries to reach you
becomes a booked job, and you stop being the bottleneck in your own company.* There's a
status layer available too — the operator who's always reachable wins the review war
against competitors who aren't.

Sharpen to something like: **"Every call answered. Every job booked. Even when you're
under a house at 8pm."**

**Perceived Likelihood — this is your biggest available gain.** The single most powerful
asset this business can build is a **recording of the prospect's own phone going
unanswered**. Not a claim about missed calls in general — *their* phone, *last Tuesday at
7:40pm*, ringing out. That is proof in the only form that can't be argued with, and it's
free to produce.

Second: the existing `calculator.html` and `scorecard.html` are likelihood instruments,
not lead-gen toys. Treat them that way. A calculator that takes their real call volume
and their real average ticket and returns a dollar figure converts skepticism into
arithmetic.

Third: case studies must be **same trade, same county, named**. One Sarasota HVAC
company with a real number beats a page of generic testimonials.

**Time Delay — strong, and under-marketed.** This product can be live in under 48 hours
and can recover a real job in week one. That is a genuinely excellent time-delay profile
and it should be in the headline, not buried on a features page. "Live Thursday.
First recovered job by Monday."

**Effort & Sacrifice — strong, and also under-marketed.** The critical sentence for this
market is: *"You don't change anything. You keep your number, your phone, and your
process. We sit behind it."* Tradespeople are rationally terrified of a tech change
breaking the phone line that is their entire revenue. Address that fear explicitly and by
name — the fear is the sacrifice, and naming it removes it.

**Net:** the two denominator terms are already strong and just need to be *said*. The
numerator is where the work is — specifically the likelihood term, and specifically
through proof drawn from the prospect's own business.
